##  1.3 - Old Technology vs. New Technology
    
In this lesson, we are going to look at the old and new technology found in Alabama networks. Click through the slides to learn more.

Modern Need

Fast internet is something we need now. We need it to work, to communicate, to keep us safe. The problem is how to do it? How to get fast internet to everyone, no matter where they live?

---

Modern Problem

How do phone, internet, and cable companies send Alabamians the most data in the fastest, most efficient way?

---

Modern Solution

Send data on **two types of cables** - **Ethernet** and **Fiber Optic**. 

![alt text](image-9.png)

---

Alabama Networks

**Fiber optic cable is definitely the future**, but most networks you’ll work on in Alabama will have a ***combination of older ethernet and newer fiber optic cables.***

---

**Ethernet Cable**

Ethernet cable technology has been around since the 80’s. Copper wires are bundled together to create a cable. Electrical signals are sent down the copper wires, and the wires can be twisted together to make new connections. **These signals will move quickly over short distances, but weaken the farther they travel.** They work great covering short spans, like room-to-room inside buildings and homes. These cables can be easily cut and reconnected with simple tools like pliers and wire strippers.

> - Ethernet works great covering short spans like room-to-room

![alt text](image-10.png)


On the job, you’ll hear these cables called **CAT5, CAT6, or CAT7**. We’re always improving our technology, so these are just names for different generations, or categories, of ethernet cable as they get faster and faster. You’ll see these cables on power lines, and they can also be buried underground. Most of the wiring inside homes and offices is ethernet cable. These cables have plugs that are very recognizable, and most devices we use come with ethernet receptors built into them.

**Fiber Optic Cable**

Fiber Optic cables are different. Instead of copper wire, the core of this cable is a tiny glass or plastic tube that’s the **thickness of a human hair**. Because these fibers are so thin, they are lighter than ethernet and many more of them can be bundled together into a single cable. Instead of electricity, data moves through these cables using pulses of light. **Light travels so much faster than electricity**, it’s not even close! It’s like comparing a little league football team to the SEC champion. **A single fiber optic cable can run up to 60 miles** before experiencing signal loss! This makes fiber better for covering long distances between data sources and rural areas.

![alt text](image-11.png)

**Fiber cable is more complex to cut and connect**, though. It **requires a lot of practice**, and **equipment that costs thousands of dollars**. There are different types of fiber optic cable, but on the job, you’ll just hear it called “Fiber.” Fiber cable is also run on power lines, usually below ethernet cable, and it can also be buried underground. Since this is newer technology, ***you’ll need an adapter to connect a fiber cable to a common device like the modem from your cable company.***


> ## 🧵 What the sentence means
> 
> It means that **fiber-optic cable can be installed overhead on the same utility poles that carry electrical power lines**.
> 
> In the usual arrangement, the fiber cable is attached **lower on the pole than the energized electrical conductors**. The course’s phrase “below Ethernet cable” is likely imprecise shorthand for *below other communications cables*—or it may simply be mistaken, because Ethernet is normally a local network cable inside a building, not a typical utility-pole cable.
> 
> ## ⚡ Power lines vs. fiber
> 
> A common U.S. utility pole has vertical “zones”:
> 
> | Pole location | Usually carries | Why it is there |
> |---|---|---|
> | Top | High-voltage primary power conductors | Keeps the most hazardous conductors highest and separated. |
> | Middle | Lower-voltage electrical equipment/conductors, neutral, transformers | Electrical distribution to nearby buildings. |
> | Safety separation zone | Required empty vertical space | Helps protect communications workers from contact with energized power conductors. |
> | Lower portion | Telephone, cable-TV coax, and fiber-optic cable | This is the communications space. |
> 
> Fiber is generally categorized as a **communications line**, so it is normally placed in the communications space below the power/supply space. Safety standards require separation between the highest communications attachment and the electrical conductors above it; the exact clearance depends on the electrical configuration and installation details. [standards.ieee](https://standards.ieee.org/wp-content/uploads/import/documents/interpretations/ir571.pdf)
> 
> ## 🌐 Why fiber can be near power
> 
> Fiber sends data as **pulses of light through glass or plastic**, not as electrical current through copper conductors.
> 
> That provides two important advantages:
> 
> - Fiber itself does not carry normal network electrical signals, so it is not susceptible to electromagnetic interference from nearby power lines in the way copper data cables can be.
> - Many aerial fiber designs are **all-dielectric**—they contain no conductive metal—making them suitable for installation near electrical infrastructure when installed under the applicable engineering and safety rules. [thefoa](https://thefoa.org/tech/ref/appln/ElecUtil.html)
> 
> That does **not** mean anyone can hang fiber wherever they want. Shared utility poles are carefully engineered for clearance, mechanical loading, grounding/bonding where applicable, worker safety, sag between poles, wind, ice, and local utility requirements.
> 
> ## 🧱 What “run on power lines” can mean
> 
> The wording can refer to either of two different installations:
> 
> 1. **Most common: same poles, below the power wires.**  
>    Fiber is suspended from a messenger wire or uses a self-supporting cable, attached to the lower communications portion of utility poles. This is probably what your course means. [ecmag](https://www.ecmag.com/magazine/articles/article-detail/integrated-systems-fiber-technology-electrical-utilities-techniques-installing-fiber-power)
> 
> 2. **Specialized utility installation: directly on or within power-line infrastructure.**  
>    Utilities can use specially designed fiber systems—such as optical power attached cable or fiber integrated with ground/shield wire—to connect substations and utility equipment. These are not ordinary residential ISP installations and require specialized equipment and trained electrical/fiber crews. [thefoa](https://thefoa.org/tech/ref/appln/ElecUtil.html)
> 
> ## ✅ Plain-English rewrite
> 
> A more precise version of the course statement would be:
> 
> > “Aerial fiber-optic cable may be installed on utility poles that also carry electrical power lines. In typical joint-use pole installations, the fiber is placed in the lower communications space, beneath the power conductors and with required safety clearance.”
> 
> ## 🧠 Key correction: “Ethernet cable”
> 
> “Ethernet cable” usually means a copper twisted-pair cable such as Cat5e, Cat6, or Cat6A, generally used **inside homes, offices, and buildings**. It is not normally the cable being referenced when discussing lines strung between utility poles.
> 
> The likely intended wording was one of these:
> 
> - “Fiber cable is commonly run **below power lines**.”
> - “Fiber cable may be run below **telephone and cable-TV lines**.”
> - “Fiber cable is installed in the **communications space** on a shared utility pole.”
> 
> So, at the precise level: **fiber is not ordinarily run “below Ethernet cable”; it is ordinarily attached below electrical supply conductors on a shared utility pole.**

**Now that you have learned a little bit about ethernet cables and fiber optic cables, let's review how they are similar and different.** 

## **How are they similar?**

The main similarity between ethernet and fiber is the physical location of the cabling. **Both ethernet cable and fiber optic cables can be hung on power poles or can be buried underground.**

## **How are they different?**

The main differences between ethernet and fiber are shown in the table below. 

  

| Old Technology  <br>Ethernet Cable | New Technology  <br>Fiber Optic Cable |
| --- | --- |
| made of copper wire,  bundled into a cable | made of tiny glass or plastic tubes, bundled into a cable |
| data moves using electricity | data moves using light beam pulses  <br>(light is faster than electricity) |
| cut and connect with simple tools | cut and connect with expensive, professional tools |
| familiar plugs work with most devices | requires adapters to plug into most devices |
| 100-foot max run before signal loss | 60-mile max run before signal loss |


> ## 🟢 Yes — this clarification changes it
> 
> With the full lesson text, the course is saying that **both copper Ethernet cabling and fiber-optic cabling can be installed outdoors in two broad physical pathways**:
> 
> 1. **Aerial** — suspended above ground on poles or other supports.
> 2. **Underground** — placed in conduit/duct, or sometimes designed for direct burial.
> 
> So the intended “similarity” is about **where the cable may be installed**, not how the two cable types transmit data.
> 
> ## 🧵 What “Ethernet cable” means here
> 
> In this course, “Ethernet cable” almost certainly means **copper twisted-pair network cable**, such as Cat5e, Cat6, or Cat6A—not necessarily a short patch cable between a PC and a switch.
> 
> There are special outdoor-rated copper Ethernet cables made for outside-plant use. Depending on the cable design and the installation plan, these can be:
> 
> - Installed in underground conduit.
> - Direct-buried if the cable is specifically rated for direct burial.
> - Run aerially between buildings or poles when it is an appropriate outdoor/aerial design, often lashed to a supporting messenger wire.
> - Installed in ways that protect it from sunlight, water, temperature changes, physical damage, and mechanical tension. [commscope](https://www.commscope.com/globalassets/digizuite/53956-customer-owned-copper-osp-guide-co-112386-en-3-7-18-pdf.pdf)
> 
> Likewise, outside-plant fiber can be aerial, installed in conduit, or direct-buried. Aerial fiber might be lashed to a messenger wire, use a Figure-8 cable with an integrated messenger, or be all-dielectric self-supporting (ADSS). [thefoa](https://www.thefoa.org/tech/ref/OSP/cable.html)
> 
> ## ⚠️ The important practical nuance
> 
> The statement is **technically true but overly broad**.
> 
> It does *not* mean:
> 
> > “Any normal indoor Cat5e/Cat6 Ethernet patch cable can be hung from a power pole or buried in dirt.”
> 
> That would be incorrect and unsafe. Indoor Ethernet cable is generally not made to withstand UV exposure, moisture, tension from long aerial spans, weather, or burial conditions.
> 
> It means:
> 
> > “If the proper outdoor-rated cable and approved installation method are used, both copper Ethernet cabling and fiber-optic cabling can travel above ground on poles or below ground.”
> 
> Aerial telecommunications installations can support optical fiber, balanced twisted-pair copper, and coaxial cable. [bicsi](https://www.bicsi.org/docs/default-source/publications/bicsi-g2-2-22-preview.pdf?sfvrsn=26d1ad7_8)
> 
> ## ⚡ “On power poles” does not mean “on power wires”
> 
> The wording **“hung on power poles”** means the cable is attached to the *pole structure*, not attached directly to energized power conductors.
> 
> A simplified shared utility-pole layout is:
> 
> ```text
>               Top of pole
>                    │
>        Electrical power conductors
>                    │
>           Electrical equipment
>                    │
>        Required safety separation
>                    │
>    Communications space:
>    • Telephone copper
>    • Cable-TV coax
>    • Fiber optic cable
>    • In some cases, outdoor-rated
>      twisted-pair Ethernet/data cable
>                    │
>               Ground level
> ```
> 
> The electrical supply conductors occupy the upper part of the pole. Communications cables—including fiber and, where deployed, outdoor-rated copper data cable—are placed lower down with specified clearance, engineering, and utility permission.
> 
> ## 🔍 How they differ
> 
> The next course table is likely comparing the actual transmission media, not physical location:
> 
> | Feature | Copper Ethernet cable | Fiber-optic cable |
> |---|---|---|
> | Signal type | Electrical signals | Pulses of light |
> | Main material | Copper conductors | Glass or plastic optical fibers |
> | Electrical interference | Can be affected by EMI/RFI, especially near electrical equipment | Immune to electromagnetic interference |
> | Distance | Standard Ethernet over twisted-pair copper is commonly limited to 100 m per channel/link segment | Can operate over much longer distances, depending on fiber type and optics |
> | Electricity | Conductive; can create grounding, lightning, surge, and electrical-potential concerns outdoors | The fiber strand itself is nonconductive; some fiber cable designs may still contain metal strength members |
> | Typical role | Device-to-switch and short building/campus runs | Building backbone, campus links, ISP networks, and longer/high-bandwidth links |
> 
> For example, if two school buildings are 60 meters apart, outdoor-rated copper Ethernet *could* work technically if installed correctly. But for a 500-meter building-to-building link—or a link in an electrically noisy area—fiber is generally the better fit because it supports longer runs and does not conduct electrical current.
> 
> ## ✅ Best study-note wording
> 
> Write the course point this way:
> 
> > **Similarity:** Both copper Ethernet cable and fiber-optic cable can be used outdoors and installed either aerially on utility/support poles or underground, provided the cable is rated for the environment and installed using the proper method.
> 
> > **Important qualification:** “Hung on power poles” means attached to a utility pole in the communications space, not connected to or hung from energized electrical power lines.
> 
> > **Key difference:** Copper Ethernet transmits electrical signals over copper conductors; fiber transmits light through optical strands.

##  1.6 - Single Mode and Multi Mode
    
![alt text](image-31.png)

There are **two kinds of optical fiber**: **multi mode** and **single mode**. ***Multi mode is an older technology with a larger core***, and more room for light to bounce around as it reflects off of the cladding. You won't see much multi mode fiber on the job, but you need to know it exists. What you will work with **most often is single mode fiber**. Single mode fiber has a much smaller core, and this means there is **less room for light to bounce around and therefore lose strength**.  

| **Single Mode** | Multi Mode |
| --- | --- |
| *   the outer jacket color is **yellow**<br>*   can transmit up to **6.2 miles**<br>*   used for running Internet to homes  and businesses | *   the outer jacket color is **blue**<br>*   will only work at a max of **1800 feet**<br>*   used inside of buildings to tie equipment together |


##  1.7 - Spectrum of Light
    

One question you might have at this point is, “Will I be able to see these pulses of light that are pushing data through the core?” The answer is, “No.” There are two reasons you will not be able to see the light in the core:

1.  The core is covered by layers of protective materials (e.g., cladding, jacket).
2.  The light pulsing inside the core is in the **infrared (IR) zone** of the spectrum of electromagnetic radiation. IR light is outside of the range of wavelengths humans can see.

## **Infrared Zone**

There are **specific wavelengths** within the infrared zone that **produce the highest quality fiber optic signals**, so these are the wavelengths that fiber equipment uses to send data.  Think about these like **old school radio channels**. To hear the best audio, you have to dial into the right channel. You can’t hear the song on the radio clearly until you dial the exact channel.  Same thing with fiber optics. The best stations for fiber optic transmission are 1310 nm, 1490 nm, 1550 nm, and 1610 nm. That’s why all fiber equipment is calibrated to run on these frequencies.  You’ll learn more about these frequencies when we dive deeper into testing fiber optic networks.

## Passive Optical Network (PON)

The most common type of network that serves Alabama communities is a Passive Optical Network or PON. This type of network is specifically designed for transmitting video, data, and voice traffic is called a PON, short for Passive Optical Network.  A PON moves data signals using light and reflection instead of electrical power. 

**P** - Passive (***no electricity required***)

**O** - Optical (powered by reflected light) 

**N** - Network (a group of interconnected things)


> - **passive optical network (PON):** the most common type of network that serves Alabama communities; specifically designed to transmit video, data, and voice traffic; moves data signals using light and reflection instead of electrical power
